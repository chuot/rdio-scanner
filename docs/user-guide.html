Scanner Webapp Guide

Version 8.0.0. This guide covers the webapp a Rdio Scanner server serves to the people who listen to it: the main screen and what each part of it says, how live calls arrive and how to choose which ones to hear, how to search the calls the server has kept, what an access code does and what it does not, what the application does on a phone or a tablet, how it behaves for a keyboard and a screen reader, and the reference tables at the end. The guide links to nothing outside itself: every cross-reference is a chapter of this guide. The server itself — installing it, configuring it, and the administration interface that does that — is the subject of the administrator guide, which is named in prose wherever a listener needs an operator's help.

1 · The scanner webapp, and this guide

Rdio Scanner is a server that collects the recordings a software-defined radio recorder writes, works out which system, site, talkgroup, and unit each recording came from, keeps them, and hands them to the people who listen. This guide is about the part you listen with: the page the server serves at its own root address, where calls arrive as they happen, where you choose what you want to hear, and where you search the calls the server has already kept.

It is one page, it needs nothing installed, and it is kept in your browser — so it opens even when the server cannot be reached. What it can show you with no server is a chapter of its own, and so is what happens on a phone or a tablet.

How the name is said

Rdio Scanner /ˈreɪ.di.oʊ ˈskæn.ər/ RAY-dee-oh SCAN-er

The name is written Rdio and spoken Raydio. The written name is the one on the screen and in this guide; the spoken name is the one to use when you have to ask an operator about a call, because saying it out loud is enough for them to know which product you mean.

Who this guide is for

It is written for the person who listens, and for the operator who hands it to them. Everything in it is written from the listening side of the deployment: what a control does when you press it, what the screen says when something has gone wrong, and what you can and cannot settle yourself. Nothing in it asks you to configure a server, and nothing in it assumes you can. Where a thing can only be settled by the operator who runs the server, the guide says so and says what to tell them.

What this guide does not cover

The server itself is not covered here: installing it, meeting its requirements, configuring what it records and how long it keeps it, driving its administration interface, and driving its APIs. The administration interface lives at a separate address of the same server — the operator's own screens, which the server names /admin when it starts — and the administrator guide, published beside this one, covers all of it. Where a listener needs the operator's guide, this guide names it in words rather than as a file, because the copy you are reading may be all you have.

The native applications for Android and Apple are named here and not described. The listening application offers them on a small screen, which is described in the chapter about phones and tablets; the applications in the stores have their own screens, their own settings, and their own maintainers, and this guide documents none of them and links to no store.

What the update costs you

The update is the operator's, not yours, and there is nothing for you to install. What it costs a listener is small and worth knowing in advance, because it is the kind of thing that looks like a broken deployment on the day it happens:

  • The operator's database is upgraded in place. It is the same database, with its recorded calls, at the same address; it is not copied somewhere new.
  • Every administration session that was open when the update ran ends. That is the operator's screens, not your listening.
  • A stored access code may have to be presented again. If the screen opens straight to the unlock screen after an update, that is why, and the code is the one the operator gave you.
  • A saved selection may need clearing. The selection the application kept in your browser can survive the update in a shape the new application does not recognise, and /reset, which the chapter on phones and tablets describes, puts it back to a starting point.

2 · The main screen

Everything you do with the listening application happens on one screen. It is built in three areas, and they sit in the same order on every device: the branding line with the server light beside it, the display area, and the control area. Learn where things are on this page and every later chapter is short.

The branding line, and the server light

The branding line is the operator's own words for the deployment: the name of the department, the name of the scanner network, or nothing at all. When the operator sets nothing the line is empty, and the server light sits at the left end of where the line would have been. The line tells you which deployment you are looking at, which is the first thing to check when two deployments look the same.

The light is the quickest indication on the screen. It has three states, and they differ by colour, glow, and motion rather than by shape: the same square is dark, lit, or lit inside a ring.

  • Dark while nothing is playing and nothing has just played. The application is not receiving a call.
  • Lit while a call is playing, in the colour that call's talkgroup resolves to — which comes from a group, a tag, the system, or the talkgroup itself, in that order of being looked at first.
  • Paused while audio is held: the lit square inside a ring. Normally the ring blinks. When your operating system asks for less motion, the ring stands still instead, and carries the same meaning.

The page also says each state out loud for a screen reader, as Server status: followed by the state — off, on, or on paused — so the state is not only a colour you have to look at.

If you cannot rely on colour

The paused state is carried by the ring as well as by the colour, and the lit state is carried by a glow as well as by the hue. The colours themselves are not reliable enough on their own, and neither is the glow: the chapter on keyboards and screen readers gives the measured figures and says what the screen still leaves to colour alone.

The display area

At the top of the display, three readings sit in one line: the time on your machine, the state of the page's connection to the server, and the queue count. The connection reads NO LINK until the page has joined the server, and once joined it reads L: followed by the number of other listeners when the operator has chosen to show that number, and nothing at all when the operator has not. What to do when it stays at NO LINK is in the chapter about when something does not work.

Below that line sit the rows that describe a call: the system, the talkgroup's type, the tag, the talkgroup, the date and the running clock of the call, the call's name in the largest type on the page, the frequency, the talkgroup identifier, the statistics line, and the unit that spoke. Before a first call has arrived, each row holds the placeholder it was built with — System, Tag, Talkgroup, TGID: 0, E: 0 S: 0, UID: 0, a frequency written as a run of zero digits, and 00:00 in the place where a call's running clock will appear. The row that carries the clock shows the time on your machine the whole time, placeholder or not.

The name row is what tells you the version you are listening with. Before the first call arrives it reads Rdio Scanner followed by the version and the build the operator's server is running. Once a call has played, that row carries call names from then on, and the last call's rows stay on the screen after the call ends; the version is not shown again until the page is reloaded. This guide asks you to read that line to the operator when something is missing, and the chapter about help and support says how.

Underneath the call rows sits the list of recent calls, with its own column headings — Time, System, Talkgroup, Name — and the flags that appear beside them when a call carries something extra: AVOID, PATCH, DELAYED, and the hourglass with the minutes left on an avoid you armed. Every row, and what each one holds, is given one at a time in the chapter about what the display says.

3 · Listening to live calls

Listening is what the application does once you start it. The server sends the calls its recorder is capturing, the display lights for the one now playing, and the controls decide what you are offered next. One press on LIVE FEED is what sets the listening going: the page opens with the feed off, and it stays off until you press, even when your selection is already saved and the server is already recording.

The live feed control

LIVE FEED decides whether the application is offering you anything at all. Like the other controls that carry a state, it has a small light in the upper right corner of its face. That light is present in every state and is always blinking, so the state is told by its colour alone: red for off, green for live, yellow for a search holding the feed. Nothing else on the control says which state it is in, and the chapter on keyboards and screen readers gives the measured figures for those colours and says what a reader who cannot tell them apart has to work from.

The three states of the live feed control, what each one means, and where this guide operates it.
The control's light What it means Where this guide operates it
Red The feed is off. The server is still recording, and your selection is still saved, but nothing is being offered to you, and the calls that were waiting to be played are dropped when you switch the feed off. This chapter
Green Live. Calls play as they arrive, and your selection is sent to the server so it knows which calls to send you. This chapter
Yellow A search is holding the feed: the calls you searched are being played one after another, and the live stream is not what is being played. Searching recorded calls

A press moves between those states, but not in the order the states are arranged in. From off, a press starts the live feed. From live, a press stops it. From the state a search leaves the feed in, a press ends that playback and leaves the feed off, and a second press is needed before live calls come back. That second press is the thing a listener reaches for and does not find, and it is the first symptom the chapter about things not working takes up. Closing the search panel ends a searched playback the same way, and stops the audio with it.

The two counts

Q: is the number of calls waiting to be played. It does not count the call you are hearing, because a call leaves the waiting list when its tone is taken up, before its recording begins: while one call is playing and nothing else is queued, the reading is zero. A skip takes one out of the list, so the number falls as you work through what is waiting rather than climbing. The reading is taken from what is waiting at the moment the page paints it, and only a recording waits on an alert tone, never the queue, which is why a call that arrives while a tone is still sounding is already counted. When a search is holding the feed, the same reading names the searched calls that will play, including the one playing now.

L: is the number of other listeners the server can see. It appears only when the operator has chosen to show it, and when the operator has not, nothing is printed in that place at all. It is a number about the deployment, not about you: it says how many other pages are joined to the server, and it is the first thing to mention when a deployment is failing to feed people. Where it reads NO LINK instead, the page has not joined the server, and the chapter about things not working starts there.

Alert tones

An alert tone is a short sound that plays in front of a live call's recording, never over it. Four levels can carry a tone, and the application consults them in order: the groups the talkgroup belongs to, then the talkgroup's tag, then the system, then the talkgroup itself. The first of those that carries a tone name is the one used, and the rest are not consulted.

The name refers to a tone in a table the server sends with the deployment's configuration. Nine are built in, named alert1 through alert9, and each is a sequence of short tones with its own shape: alert1 is three quick high beeps, alert5 is one long hold, alert8 is three short low clicks. The operator assigns a name to a group, a tag, a system, or a talkgroup. A name the table does not carry, or an entry that is not a sequence, means the call begins with no tone in front of it, and that is worth knowing when a deployment has been configured by hand.

Only a call arriving live gets a tone. A call you bring back with REPLAY LAST, a call you reach through a search, and every call in a searched queue all begin with their recording and nothing in front of them. A deployment that assigns tones to groups answers every call on those groups with the same tone, which is usually what an operator intends and often what makes a listener notice a group at all.

The colour of the server light

The same four levels are consulted for colour, in the same order, and the first one carrying a colour wins. The colour is used for the server light beside the branding line; the rows of the display keep the display's own colour, so a call is not written out in the colour its talkgroup carries. The eight colours the application knows are blue, cyan, green, magenta, orange, red, white, and yellow. When none of the four levels carries a colour, the light takes green, which is why an unconfigured deployment and a deployment whose light is green for every call look the same from the listener's side.

A colour you cannot rely on

The light's colour is the only thing that tells you which colour was resolved, and the measured contrast of some of those colours is not sufficient. If you tell calls apart by their light, read the chapter on keyboards and screen readers, which gives the figures and says what the light carries besides colour.

The two holds

HOLD SYS and HOLD TG narrow what you are hearing down to the call in front of you. HOLD SYS keeps the system that call came from switched on and switches off every system other than it; if the system you are hearing was switched off as a whole, it comes on. HOLD TG keeps only that one talkgroup and switches off everything else, including the rest of its own system. Both act on the call now playing, and when nothing is playing they act on the last call that played, which is how a hold is still useful a moment after a call has finished.

The application keeps what your selection was before the hold. Press the same control a second time and what you had selected is put back as it was, which is the reason a hold is safe to try: it is reversible by the same press. Press HOLD SYS while a talkgroup hold is standing and the talkgroup hold is released first, then the system is held. Press either control when nothing has played yet and the press is refused with the refusal tone, and nothing about your selection changes.

A hold is not only a filter in your browser. Your selection is sent to the server, which stops sending what the selection excludes, and calls that were already waiting and would not be accepted by the new selection are dropped from the waiting list. When you put your selection back, the server starts sending the rest again; what it does not do is give back the calls that arrived while the hold was standing.

Replay last

REPLAY LAST plays again the call that most recently finished. Press it a second time within a second and it walks one call further back through the recent calls, and a third press one further back after that; the walk starts over from the most recent call a second after your last press. It is refused with the refusal tone while audio is paused, and refused the same way when nothing has played yet. The recent calls it walks through are the list under the display, and the chapter about what the display says gives how far back that list goes.

Skip next

SKIP NEXT stops the recording that is playing and moves on to the next call in the waiting list. It does not clear the list, and it does not silence the deployment: what is coming is still coming. Pressed when nothing is waiting it still answers with the activation tone, which can read as a promise the page cannot keep. When a search is holding the feed, the same control advances the walk through the searched calls; the short gap that sits between one searched call and the next belongs to the walk itself, and the chapter about searching recorded calls gives it.

Avoid, and the windows it keeps

AVOID holds off the talkgroup of the call in front of you, and a press does not choose a length: it walks the same talkgroup through a fixed cycle. The cycle is the same one the older application had, and it is worth learning as a sequence rather than reading it off the screen each time.

What each press of AVOID does to the talkgroup of the call in front of you, in order.
Press What that talkgroup does afterwards
First Held off indefinitely: no time limit, held for as long as you leave it.
Second Held off for 30 minutes.
Third Held off for 60 minutes.
Fourth Held off for 120 minutes.
Fifth You are hearing it again.

A timed window opens itself again when it runs out; you do not have to remember to press anything, and the talkgroup is hearing again on its own. While a window is standing, the display shows the hourglass mark with the minutes left beside it, and the select panel marks that talkgroup with the same timed reading — which is how you find out that a talkgroup is being held off when it is not the call in front of you. The chapter about choosing what to hear gives that mark, and it also gives the one thing that clears a window armed here: a plain press on that talkgroup in the select panel.

AVOID needs a call to act on. Pressed when nothing has played yet it is refused with the refusal tone, and the refusal leaves everything as it was.

Pause

PAUSE silences the recording in flight, in the middle of the call, and freezes the call's running clock at the minute the audio stopped at. Press it again and the recording continues from where it stopped rather than restarting, which is what makes the pause worth using when a call is not for you right now. The server light carries the paused reading while the pause stands: the lit square inside a ring.

PAUSE works whether the audio is a live call, a replayed call, or a searched one. Pressed when nothing is playing it does nothing you can hear, and it leaves the control lit as though something were paused; press it again to put the control back, and note that the server light does not take the paused reading without a call to attach it to. If audio will not pause at all, or pauses and will not come back, that is the chapter about things not working.

The tones a press answers with

Every press on the control bar answers with a tone out of the beep set the operator chose for the deployment. Two sets are built in, named for two scanner makes, and which one a deployment uses is the operator's choice: a listener can hear the difference and cannot change it. A deployment configured without a beep set answers a press with no sound at all, which is worth knowing before a listener reports that the controls are silent. The tones say what the press did, and there are three of them:

  • The activation tone, played when a control that has an off state is switched on: LIVE FEED coming on, a hold taken, AVOID taken, PAUSE taken. It is also what REPLAY LAST answers with when it acts, and what SKIP NEXT and the two panel openers answer with, because those have no off state to leave.
  • The deactivation tone, played when a control that has an off state is put back: the feed going off, a hold released, AVOID released, PAUSE released.
  • The refusal tone, played when the application will not do what the press asked: either hold or AVOID pressed when nothing has played yet, and REPLAY LAST pressed while audio is paused or when nothing has played yet.

The two panel openers answer with the tone only once the deployment's configuration has reached the page. Before it arrives, SEARCH CALL and SELECT TG do nothing and play nothing, which is how you can tell the page has not yet been told what the deployment contains.

The prompt when you try to leave

While the application is offering you anything — live calls, or a queue a search made — it asks your browser to confirm before you leave the page. The application does not draw a dialog of its own; it hands your browser the words it wants used, and your browser shows its own warning, sometimes in its own wording. The words the application supplies are Live Feed is ON, do you really want to leave?

The sentence names the live feed even when what is holding the page is a searched queue rather than the live stream, and that is the application's wording rather than a description of your state: what it is checking is whether it is offering you anything. Leaving the page ends what the page was doing for you: the waiting calls are dropped, and what you had selected is kept for the next time you open the page.

The display dimmer

The display starts dimmed, and it is not a fault: it lights when a call arrives, and dims again five seconds after that. A press on the controls that change what the display is showing lights it the same way — the feed, the two holds, REPLAY LAST, SKIP NEXT, PAUSE, and AVOID when there is a call to avoid. The two panel openers do not light it, and neither does a refused AVOID: pressing SEARCH CALL on a dimmed display leaves it dimmed.

Five seconds is fixed. The older application had a setting for how long the display stayed lit, and that setting is gone; a listener looking for it in the panels will not find it, and there is nothing on the listener's side to change.

1 · The scanner webapp, and this guide

Rdio Scanner is a server that collects the recordings a software-defined radio recorder writes, works out which system, site, talkgroup, and unit each recording came from, keeps them, and hands them to the people who listen. This guide is about the part you listen with: the page the server serves at its own root address, where calls arrive as they happen, where you choose what you want to hear, and where you search the calls the server has already kept.

It is one page, it needs nothing installed, and it is kept in your browser — so it opens even when the server cannot be reached. What it can show you with no server is a chapter of its own, and so is what happens on a phone or a tablet.

How the name is said

Rdio Scanner /ˈreɪ.di.oʊ ˈskæn.ər/ RAY-dee-oh SCAN-er

The name is written Rdio and spoken Raydio. The written name is the one on the screen and in this guide; the spoken name is the one to use when you have to ask an operator about a call, because saying it out loud is enough for them to know which product you mean.

Who this guide is for

It is written for the person who listens, and for the operator who hands it to them. Everything in it is written from the listening side of the deployment: what a control does when you press it, what the screen says when something has gone wrong, and what you can and cannot settle yourself. Nothing in it asks you to configure a server, and nothing in it assumes you can. Where a thing can only be settled by the operator who runs the server, the guide says so and says what to tell them.

What this guide does not cover

The server itself is not covered here: installing it, meeting its requirements, configuring what it records and how long it keeps it, driving its administration interface, and driving its APIs. The administration interface lives at a separate address of the same server — the operator's own screens, which the server names /admin when it starts — and the administrator guide, published beside this one, covers all of it. Where a listener needs the operator's guide, this guide names it in words rather than as a file, because the copy you are reading may be all you have.

The native applications for Android and Apple are named here and not described. The listening application offers them on a small screen, which is described in the chapter about phones and tablets; the applications in the stores have their own screens, their own settings, and their own maintainers, and this guide documents none of them and links to no store.

What the update costs you

The update is the operator's, not yours, and there is nothing for you to install. What it costs a listener is small and worth knowing in advance, because it is the kind of thing that looks like a broken deployment on the day it happens:

  • The operator's database is upgraded in place. It is the same database, with its recorded calls, at the same address; it is not copied somewhere new.
  • Every administration session that was open when the update ran ends. That is the operator's screens, not your listening.
  • A stored access code is usually presented again by itself, because the newer application reads the copy the older one left in your browser. When the screen opens straight to the unlock screen after an update, that copy is not answering, and the code to type is the one the operator gave you.
  • A saved selection may need clearing. The selection the application kept in your browser can survive the update in a shape the new application does not recognise, and /reset, which the chapter on phones and tablets describes, puts it back to a starting point.

The branding line, and the server light

The branding line is the operator's own words for the deployment: the name of the department, the name of the scanner network, or nothing at all. When the operator sets nothing the line is empty, and the server light sits at the left end of where the line would have been. The line tells you which deployment you are looking at, which is the first thing to check when two deployments look the same.

The light is the quickest indication on the screen. It has three states, and they differ by colour, glow, and motion rather than by shape: the same square is dark, lit, or lit inside a ring.

  • Dark while nothing is playing and nothing has just played. The application is not receiving a call.
  • Lit while a call is playing, in the colour that call's talkgroup resolves to — which comes from a group, a tag, the system, or the talkgroup itself, in that order of being looked at first.
  • Paused while audio is held: the light alternates between the colour it is lit with and the dark grey it wears at rest, on a cycle that switches rather than fades. When your operating system asks for less motion, the alternation stops and a ring drawn standing around the lit face takes over the meaning — the ring the figure below was photographed in.

The page also says each state out loud for a screen reader, as Server status: followed by the state — off, on, or on paused — so the state is not only a colour you have to look at.

If you cannot rely on colour

The paused state is carried by the ring as well as by the colour, and the lit state is carried by a glow as well as by the hue. The colours themselves are not reliable enough on their own, and neither is the glow: the chapter on keyboards and screen readers gives the measured figures and says what the screen still leaves to colour alone.

The display area

At the top of the display, three readings sit in one line: the time on your machine, the state of the page's connection to the server, and the queue count. The connection reads NO LINK until the page has joined the server, and once joined it reads L: followed by the number of other listeners when the operator has chosen to show that number, and nothing at all when the operator has not. What to do when it stays at NO LINK is in the chapter about when something does not work.

Below that line sit the rows that describe a call: the system, the talkgroup's type, the tag, the talkgroup, the date and the running clock of the call, the call's name in the largest type on the page, the frequency, the talkgroup identifier, the statistics line, and the unit that spoke. Before a first call has arrived, each row holds the placeholder it was built with — System, Tag, Talkgroup, TGID: 0, E: 0 S: 0, UID: 0, a frequency written as a run of zero digits, and 00:00 in the place where a call's running clock will appear. The row that carries the clock shows the time on your machine the whole time, placeholder or not.

The name row is what tells you the version you are listening with. Before the first call arrives it reads Rdio Scanner followed by the version and the build the operator's server is running. Once a call has played, that row carries call names from then on, and the last call's rows stay on the screen after the call ends; the version is not shown again until the page is reloaded. This guide asks you to read that line to the operator when something is missing, and the chapter about help and support says how.

Underneath the call rows sits the list of recent calls, with its own column headings — Time, System, Talkgroup, Name — and the flags that appear beside them when a call carries something extra: AVOID, PATCH, DELAYED, and the hourglass marking an avoid you armed, with the minutes that window is held for. Every row, and what each one holds, is given one at a time in the chapter about what the display says.

3 · Listening to live calls

Listening is what the application does once you start it. The server sends the calls its recorder is capturing, the display lights for the one now playing, and the controls decide what you are offered next. One press on LIVE FEED is what sets the listening going: the page opens with the feed off, and it stays off until you press, even when your selection is already saved and the server is already recording.

The live feed control

LIVE FEED decides whether the application is offering you anything at all. Like the other controls that carry a state, it has a small light in the upper right corner of its face. That light is present in every state and its shape never changes, so the state is told by its colour alone: red for off, green for live, yellow for a search holding the feed. Nothing else on the control says which state it is in, and the chapter on keyboards and screen readers gives the measured figures for those colours and says what a reader who cannot tell them apart has to work from.

A press moves between those states, but not in the order the states are arranged in. From off, a press starts the live feed. From live, a press stops it. From the state a search leaves the feed in, a press ends that playback and leaves the feed off, and a second press is needed before live calls come back. That second press is the thing a listener reaches for and does not find, and it is the first symptom the chapter about things not working takes up. Pressing SEARCH CALL again while that panel is already open does nothing to a searched playback, and neither does closing it: the searched calls go on playing with the panel shut. What ends a searched playback from inside the panel is a change to one of its filters, or a press on its Reset, and the chapter on searching recorded calls gives both.

The two counts

Q: is the number of calls waiting to be played. It does not count the call you are hearing, because a call leaves the waiting list when its tone is taken up, before its recording begins: while one call is playing and nothing else is queued, the reading is zero. A skip takes one out of the list, so the number falls as you work through what is waiting rather than climbing. The reading is taken from what is waiting at the moment the page paints it, and only a recording waits on an alert tone, never the queue, which is why a call that arrives while a tone is still sounding is already counted. When a search is holding the feed, the same reading names the searched calls that will play, including the one playing now.

L: is the number of other listeners the server can see. It appears only when the operator has chosen to show it, and when the operator has not, nothing is printed in that place at all. It is a number about the deployment, not about you: it says how many other pages are joined to the server, and it is the first thing to mention when a deployment is failing to feed people. Where it reads NO LINK instead, the page has not joined the server, and the chapter about things not working starts there.

Alert tones

An alert tone is a short sound that plays in front of a live call's recording, never over it. Four levels can carry a tone, and the application consults them in order: the groups the talkgroup belongs to, then the talkgroup's tag, then the system, then the talkgroup itself. The first of those that carries a tone name is the one used, and the rest are not consulted.

The name refers to a tone in a table the server sends with the deployment's configuration. Nine are built in, named alert1 through alert9, and each is a sequence of short tones with its own shape: alert1 is three quick high beeps, alert5 is one long hold, alert8 is three short low clicks. The operator assigns a name to a group, a tag, a system, or a talkgroup. A name the table does not carry, or an entry that is not a sequence, means the call begins with no tone in front of it, and that is worth knowing when a deployment has been configured by hand.

Only a call arriving live gets a tone. A call you bring back with REPLAY LAST, a call you reach through a search, and every call in a searched queue all begin with their recording and nothing in front of them. A deployment that assigns tones to groups answers every call on those groups with the same tone, which is usually what an operator intends and often what makes a listener notice a group at all.

A tone opens a window, and what happens inside it matters. While the tone is playing the recording has not started, and a call that is skipped, stopped, or taken out of your selection before its tone ends never starts its recording at all: the tone is the only part of that call you hear.

The colour of the server light

The same four levels are consulted for colour, in the same order, and the first one carrying a colour wins. The colour is used for the server light beside the branding line; the rows of the display keep the display's own colour, so a call is not written out in the colour its talkgroup carries. The eight colours the application knows are blue, cyan, green, magenta, orange, red, white, and yellow. When none of the four levels carries a colour, the light takes green, which is why an unconfigured deployment and a deployment whose light is green for every call look the same from the listener's side.

A colour you cannot rely on

The light's colour is the only thing that tells you which colour was resolved, and the measured contrast of some of those colours is not sufficient. If you tell calls apart by their light, read the chapter on keyboards and screen readers, which gives the figures and says what the light carries besides colour.

The two holds

HOLD SYS and HOLD TG narrow what you are hearing down to the call in front of you. HOLD SYS keeps the system that call came from switched on and switches off every system other than it; if the system you are hearing was switched off as a whole, it comes on. HOLD TG keeps only that one talkgroup and switches off everything else, including the rest of its own system. Both act on the call now playing, and when nothing is playing they act on the last call that played, which is how a hold is still useful a moment after a call has finished.

The application keeps what your selection was before the hold. Press the same control a second time and what you had selected is put back as it was, which is the reason a hold is safe to try: it is reversible by the same press. Press HOLD SYS while a talkgroup hold is standing and the talkgroup hold is released first, then the system is held. Press either control when nothing has played yet and the press is refused with the refusal tone, and nothing about your selection changes.

A hold is not only a filter in your browser. Your selection is sent to the server, which stops sending what the selection excludes, and calls that were already waiting and would not be accepted by the new selection are dropped from the waiting list. When you put your selection back, the server starts sending the rest again; what it does not do is give back the calls that arrived while the hold was standing.

Replay last

REPLAY LAST plays again the call that most recently finished. Press it a second time within a second and it walks one call further back through the recent calls, and a third press one further back after that; the walk starts over from the most recent call a second after your last press. It is refused with the refusal tone while audio is paused, and refused the same way when nothing has played yet. The recent calls it walks through are the list under the display, and the chapter about what the display says gives how far back that list goes.

Skip next

SKIP NEXT stops the recording that is playing and moves on to the next call in the waiting list. It does not clear the list, and it does not silence the deployment: what is coming is still coming. Pressed when nothing is waiting it still answers with the activation tone, which can read as a promise the page cannot keep. When a search is holding the feed, the same control advances the walk through the searched calls; the short gap that sits between one searched call and the next belongs to the walk itself, and the chapter about searching recorded calls gives it.

Avoid, and the windows it keeps

AVOID holds off the talkgroup of the call in front of you, and a press does not choose a length: it walks the same talkgroup through a fixed cycle. The cycle is the same one the older application had, and it is worth learning as a sequence rather than reading it off the screen each time.

Pause

PAUSE silences the recording in flight, in the middle of the call, and freezes the call's running clock at the minute the audio stopped at. Press it again and the recording continues from where it stopped rather than restarting, which is what makes the pause worth using when a call is not for you right now. The server light carries the paused reading while the pause stands: the lit square inside a ring.

PAUSE works whether the audio is a live call, a replayed call, or a searched one. Pressed when nothing is playing it does nothing you can hear, and it leaves the control lit as though something were paused; press it again to put the control back, and note that the server light does not take the paused reading without a call to attach it to. If audio will not pause at all, or pauses and will not come back, that is the chapter about things not working.

The tones a press answers with

Every press on the control bar answers with a tone out of the beep set the operator chose for the deployment. Two sets are built in, named for two scanner makes, and which one a deployment uses is the operator's choice: a listener can hear the difference and cannot change it. A deployment configured without a beep set answers a press with no sound at all, which is worth knowing before a listener reports that the controls are silent. The tones say what the press did, and there are three of them:

  • The activation tone, played when a control that has an off state is switched on: LIVE FEED coming on, a hold taken, AVOID taken, PAUSE taken. It is also what REPLAY LAST answers with when it acts, and what SKIP NEXT and the two panel openers answer with, because those have no off state to leave.
  • The deactivation tone, played when a control that has an off state is put back: the feed going off, a hold released, AVOID released, PAUSE released.
  • The refusal tone, played when the application will not do what the press asked: either hold or AVOID pressed when nothing has played yet, and REPLAY LAST pressed while audio is paused or when nothing has played yet.

The two panel openers answer with the tone only once the deployment's configuration has reached the page. Before it arrives, SEARCH CALL and SELECT TG do nothing and play nothing, which is how you can tell the page has not yet been told what the deployment contains.

The prompt when you try to leave

While the application is offering you anything — live calls, or a queue a search made — it asks your browser to confirm before you leave the page. The application does not draw a dialog of its own; it hands your browser the words it wants used, and your browser shows its own warning, sometimes in its own wording. The words the application supplies are Live Feed is ON, do you really want to leave?

The sentence names the live feed even when what is holding the page is a searched queue rather than the live stream, and that is the application's wording rather than a description of your state: what it is checking is whether it is offering you anything. Leaving the page ends what the page was doing for you: the waiting calls are dropped, and what you had selected is kept for the next time you open the page.

The display dimmer

The display starts dimmed, and it is not a fault: it lights when a call arrives, and dims again five seconds after that. A press on the controls that change what the display is showing lights it the same way — the feed, the two holds, REPLAY LAST, SKIP NEXT, PAUSE, and AVOID when there is a call to avoid. The two panel openers do not light it, and neither does a refused AVOID: pressing SEARCH CALL on a dimmed display leaves it dimmed.

Five seconds is fixed. The older application had a setting for how long the display stayed lit, and that setting is gone; a listener looking for it in the panels will not find it, and there is nothing on the listener's side to change.

4 · Choosing what to hear

The select panel is where you decide which talkgroups the deployment offers you. The SELECT TG control at the bottom right of the control bar opens it over the main display, every button in it is named for the thing it speaks for, and every button in it answers the same question: are you hearing this one or not.

How the panel is arranged

The panel's first row is built from the groups the deployment defines, and it appears only when the deployment has two or more groups. It is drawn as a box with a border and no heading of its own, so your eye groups those buttons by the line around them; a screen reader is handed a group with no name, which is one of the things the chapter on keyboards and screen readers counts against the application. A group button's name is its label followed by its state word — on, off, or partial — held as words inside the button that the page shows to a screen reader and not to your eye.

Under the group row sits one box per system, headed by that system's own name, holding its talkgroups. A system with no talkgroups is not shown at all. At the end of each box sits a pair of small buttons, OFF and ON, drawn as faces of their own in red and green. A talkgroup button carries its state in two ways: the corner light, and a name a screen reader is given as pressed or not pressed. The four state-bearing controls on the control bar do not do that, and the difference matters to a reader who cannot see the lights.

Neither pair is always there. The pair at the end of a system's box appears only when that system has more than one talkgroup: with a single talkgroup, that talkgroup's own button already says everything the pair would. The pair under the group row is there whenever the group row is.

What a press does to what is waiting

A press in the panel trims the waiting list as well as the selection. Calls already queued that the new selection would not accept are dropped, so switching a talkgroup off takes its calls out of the queue behind it and the count above the display falls with them. This is also why a press on ON does not bring back calls that arrived while you were not hearing that talkgroup: the server stopped sending them, and nothing is held aside to give them back.

What a press does to a hold

A press anywhere in the panel lets go of a hold. The chapter about live calls says a second press on HOLD SYS or HOLD TG puts your selection back exactly as it was — that works only while the panel has not been touched. Press anything in the panel while a hold is standing and the copy the hold was keeping is thrown away, the hold control goes unlit, and the narrowed selection stays where it is. Pressing the hold control after that takes a fresh hold rather than restoring anything, so the way back from there is the panel itself: a system's ON for one system's talkgroups, the pair under the group row for everything.

The tones a press answers with

The panel answers with the three tones the chapter about live calls describes. A talkgroup button answers with the tone for the change it just made: the tone for going off when you had been hearing it, the tone for going on when you had not. ON answers with the tone for turning on, OFF with the tone for turning off, whatever the state was before. A group button answers with the tone for turning off when its group was fully chosen and with the tone for turning on in every other case, which is why a group that was half-chosen answers with the tone for going on.

The timed-avoid state

When the control bar's AVOID has a talkgroup held off for a window of minutes, that talkgroup's button shows it: its corner light carries the timed mark, which is the red of a talkgroup held off blinking at its faster rate, and in a page read where the operating system asks for less motion it stands still instead. The number of minutes is not written on the button. It is given as words inside the button that the page shows to a screen reader and not to your eye — avoided for 30 minutes — which is the only place in the panel where the length of a window can be read at all. That is how you find out that a talkgroup is being held off when it is not the call in front of you.

Pressing a talkgroup that is doing this does two things at once, and the second is easy to miss: the talkgroup comes back to hearing, and the window is cleared. The same clearing happens on any press that reaches the talkgroup — its group button, its system's OFF or ON, or the pair under the group row. Nothing in the panel shortens a window or sets a different length: the 30, 60, and 120 minute windows belong to AVOID alone, and the panel only ever ends them.

A timed window does not outlive the page

A window lives only in the page you are in. It is not written into the saved selection, it is not sent to the server, and when the deployment's configuration reaches the page again every window standing at that moment is dropped. What survives is the holding-off: a talkgroup you left held off stays held off after a reload, with no window attached to it and nothing waiting to bring it back. If a talkgroup you expected to start hearing again has not, this is the first thing to check.

The panel with nothing configured

A deployment whose systems carry no talkgroups renders no boxes and no buttons at all. The panel answers in words instead, with one line: No talkgroup configured! That line is the whole of what the panel does in that state. It is also the quickest way to say to an operator that the server has been started without talkgroups, rather than that your own selection has gone wrong.

What the selection remembers

Every press writes your selection into the browser, under a name that includes the listener identifier your address carries, which is what lets two pages on the same browser hold different selections; the chapter about addresses gives that part of the address. When the page is opened again the saved selection is read back and matched against the configuration that arrives: a talkgroup the deployment still has keeps the state you left it in, a talkgroup the deployment no longer has is dropped, and a talkgroup the deployment has newly gained comes in chosen unless a group it belongs to was left off. Tags no longer have a row of their own in the panel, so a tag cannot hold a new talkgroup back. A selection saved by the older application is read once, as a fallback when the current one has nothing saved yet.

The selection is also sent to the server, but only while the live feed is on. A press made while the feed is off is written down and kept, and switching the feed on sends the whole selection at that moment — which is why a selection you changed while you were not listening can seem to take effect all at once.

5 · Searching recorded calls

The search panel is the part of the listening screen that reaches backwards. SEARCH CALL at the bottom left of the control bar opens it, and everything in it concerns calls the server has already recorded and kept.

The panel and its two halves

The panel is two blocks inside one scrolling area. The results come first: the table of calls, and under it a row holding the switch that changes what a row's button does and the paginator that says which part of the answer you are looking at. The filter form sits under that. The panel scrolls on its own, and at the window size the pictures in this guide were taken at, the Reset button at the end of the form sits below what is visible until you scroll the panel — which is what a listener's wheel is for, and what the key does in the chapter on keyboards and screen readers.

The date field does not mean this day. The bounds of the date field

The panel bounds that field to the two ends the last answer named, which are the ends of the whole match rather than of the page in front of you, so the calendar the field offers follows your last search. When the last answer was empty the panel deliberately leaves the field unbounded: an empty answer names a span the archive has nothing in, and a field held to that span reads like a search that asked for the wrong day. While a search is on its way

A search in flight holds the form. A progress bar runs along the panel, the panel marks itself busy to a screen reader, and the six criteria and Reset are all marked unavailable so that a change cannot start a second search behind the first. They are marked rather than taken away, and they keep their place in the keyboard's order, which is what lets a reader stay where they were when the answer lands. The paginator is not among them: it stays available, and a move made during a search asks the server for nothing until it steps outside the answer already in hand.

The count the search found is said once per answer, not once per redraw, and nothing is said before the first answer arrives. Before it, the panel has no number to give.

Download mode

The switch under the results carries the name Download mode, and it decides what a row's button is for. With it off, pressing a row plays that recording on the page. With it on, every row's button becomes Download and the recording arrives as a file in your browser instead of playing. Switching it does not search again: the rows you already hold are redrawn, so the page keeps its calls and only changes what pressing one of them does.

What the page plays and what Download mode saves is the deployment's own recording, and it is the converted copy when the operator has audio conversion switched on: the audio you hear and the file you keep are the same copy. The reference chapter names that setting and what it does to what you hear.

A recording on its way to you replaces that one row's button with Loading: a turning mark, unavailable to press, and it appears on one row at a time.

A searched call, and how it walks

Pressing a row's Play starts that recording. If the live feed is off, the panel is now in playback mode, and the call you pressed is the beginning of a walk through the calls the search found: when one ends, the next begins on its own, with the short gap the chapter on listening to live calls gives.

The walk goes toward the newest call in the search's results whichever way the list is sorted, which means it crosses from the page you pressed on into the pages the server has not sent yet and keeps going. When it runs out of what it has in hand it asks for the next two hundred calls and continues; running out a second time ends the walk and puts the feed back on live. There is no setting that makes a searched playback stop instead of going live: the one the older deployment offered is gone.

In playback mode the count above the display means something else than it does while the feed is live. It counts the searched calls still to play, and that count includes the call being heard now and the calls the server has not sent yet, so it can be larger than the number of rows on the page in front of you.

Pressing Play on a row while the live feed is on does not switch the feed off: that recording plays, the live calls keep arriving and joining the queue, and the panel stays in the state it was in. A paused feed does not take the play action away from a row either — the rows keep their play actions while the feed is paused, and the panel does not redraw itself when pause is pressed.

The row whose recording is playing carries Stop in place of its play action, and pressing it silences the recording and leaves the walk stalled. It does not start the next searched call — the mark that would have carried the recording into its follow-on step is taken down first, deliberately — and it does not leave playback mode either. The walk picks up again when SKIP NEXT is pressed, or when another row's play action is pressed, and from there it continues from wherever that left it.

What ends a searched playback

Changing any criterion in the form, or pressing Reset, ends it: the panel stops the playback, puts itself back on the first page, and searches again. Pressing SEARCH CALL while the panel is already open does nothing to a searched playback, and neither does closing the panel with its Close search panel control — the searched calls go on playing with the panel shut, which is how a listener can walk a list they are no longer looking at. The control bar's LIVE FEED ends it and leaves the feed off, as the chapter on listening says, and one more press is needed after that before live calls come back.

What retention and a delay do to what you can find

A search can only find what the server has kept. The operator's retention setting removes calls older than the span it names, on a pass that runs every hour, and a call that pass has taken is not findable by any route the application offers. A deployment that holds a call's audio back — a delay set on a system or a talkgroup, counted from the moment the call happened — keeps that call out of a search until its hold has run out, and when it does arrive it arrives flagged DELAYED, which is the flag the display's flag row carries; chapter 6 gives that row.

Four things a listener who upgraded may already believe

The page this guide replaces described four things about this panel that the application has never done, and a reader arriving from it has them in mind. The paginator was described as taken away during a searched playback and during a search: it is taken away in neither. A row's stop action was described as leaving a searched playback behind: it silences the recording and leaves the walk where it was. A row's play action was described as unavailable when the feed is paused: the rows keep it. And the paginator was described as moving along with the walk so that the table always held the call being heard: it never moves on its own, and a listener who wants the row in front of them presses a page themselves.

7 · Access codes and the unlock screen

An access code is the operator's way of deciding who may watch a server. A listener does not choose a code, does not extend it, and cannot give themselves a different one. What a listener can do is present the code they were given, and understand what the screen is telling them when it refuses.

When the screen asks, and when it does not

A server with no access codes configured never asks. On that kind of server the listening screen opens straight into the live feed and nothing else in this chapter applies to you. A server with one or more codes is a restricted server, and on a restricted server the page connects, is turned away, and puts the unlock form over the history area at the bottom of the display. The display rows above it are still there, still showing what they were built with. The nine control buttons stay where they are and are all marked unavailable: they are not taken off the screen, and they are not something you can press through. While the page is waiting, the server reads nothing else the page sends it.

The form is a single field, and the words on it are Unlock code. There is no button to press: the code is presented when you press Enter in the field, and nothing else on the screen will do it for you.

The three sentences the field can carry

What the unlock field says, what the server actually answered, and what is worth doing about it.
The field says What actually happened What to do
Invalid unlock code The code is not one the server has. The same sentence also appears when the code is right and the tries have run out: running out is answered exactly as a wrong guess is. Check the code against what you were given. If you have tried several times, stop trying — the sentence will not change, and nothing you type can tell you which of the two it is.

Whichever sentence appears, the code you typed stays in the field. The screen does not clear it, which is the right choice: a code with one character wrong is corrected rather than retyped. The field is also given back the keyboard, so the correction is ready to make.

The five tries, and what spending them feels like

A listener may present a code five times on one connection. Five more are counted against the address that connection came from, over a window of ten minutes, and that second counter is shared by everything else presenting codes from the same place — another tab, a phone on the same network, the administration page on the same browser. Either counter running out ends the tries. And the fifth attempt is refused even when the code it presents is the correct one: the count is a limit on trying, not a record of whether the last try was right.

Spending the budget feels exactly like being wrong. There is no sentence anywhere on the screen that says the tries are gone; the field goes on saying Invalid unlock code and nothing about it changes. A refused try is not forgiven afterwards, whichever reason refused it. A code that is accepted clears both counters — the connection's and the address's — and starts the budget again, the way a successful sign-in renews a login budget. Reloading the page does not buy a fresh start, because the reload shares the address counter with the page it replaces. Waiting for the window to pass is the only move that costs nothing.

If the sentence will not change

Stop presenting the code. Every presentation is one of the five that the address has, so a listener who keeps trying a code they are not sure of uses up the tries of everyone else working from the same place, and can lock out a colleague who was doing nothing wrong. Confirm the code with the operator instead of testing it: the operator can see the attempts in the server's own log, and can tell you in one look whether the code is wrong or the tries are gone.

The code the page keeps

A code that was accepted is kept in the browser, and the next time the page opens it presents that code again without asking. It is kept in an encoded form rather than a protected one: anyone with access to the machine can read it back. A code kept in a browser on a shared or public machine is not a secret that machine is keeping for you.

Nothing in the ordinary running of the page ever forgets it. An expired code is not forgotten, a wrong code is not forgotten, and the stored one is offered again on every reconnect. That has one consequence a listener should know about: a code the server no longer accepts can spend the address's five tries by itself, one attempt per reconnect, with nobody typing anything. If the field starts refusing a code you know is right, on a machine that has been left open, this is a thing to check, and /reset at the end of the page's address is the way to clear it.

What a code gives, and what it holds back

A code is not only a door. The deployment attaches a scope to it: the systems that code may see. A code scoped to two systems gives the listener a screen with two systems on it, and the systems it was not given are not on the screen at all — not greyed out, not folded away, not findable by searching. A listener who was told to listen to a talkgroup and cannot find it is usually holding a code that does not name that talkgroup's system, and no choice made on the selection panel will bring it back. The same limit runs through a search of recorded calls, and through fetching a recording: a code cannot reach past its own systems in either.

Before a code has been accepted there is no configuration, no live calls, and no search. The page is connected to the server and is being shown nothing. On the screen that looks like a server that is not answering, and it is not the same thing; the chapter on when something does not work takes the two apart.

When the server itself is full

A deployment caps how many connections it will hold at once. The setting is Max Clients, it starts at 200, and it counts every connection the server is holding — including ones that never got as far as becoming a listener. Over the cap the server closes the connection and sends nothing at all. No sentence appears on any field: the display keeps NO LINK and behaves as though the network had failed, which is why a full server is usually mistaken for a broken one. The field's Too many connections sentence is not this cap; it is the limit on your own code.

Several listeners on one machine

The page remembers its selection under a name taken from the address it was opened at, and the address carries a profile: adding ?id= and a word of your choosing gives that page its own profile. Two pages opened with different ?id= values keep separate saved selections, so two radios on one machine stop overwriting each other's choices, and what you change in one does not appear in the other.

A profile covers the saved selection and nothing else. The stored access code is not per profile: every profile on one browser shares the same single stored code, and so does the administration page on that browser. Two profiles are a way of keeping two selections apart, not a way of keeping two codes apart, and not a way of getting past the attempt budget, which counts the address rather than the profile.

Starting over

Putting /reset at the end of the page's address clears what the application kept in the browser — the stored access code, the saved selection, all of it — and returns you to the plain address with nothing remembered. It is the move to make when a stored code is causing trouble, or when a saved selection has to be abandoned rather than corrected.

Two things about it are worth knowing first. It clears the browser's stored data for that site rather than only the entries this application wrote, so anything else that site had stored there goes with it. And it changes nothing on the server: an address that has spent its tries has still spent them, and an expired code is still expired. If you have locked an address out, /reset gets you a clean page and you still have to wait, or get the code from the operator.

8 · On a phone or a tablet

There is one listening screen. It is not a different application on a phone, and nothing in this chapter is a feature that only exists there. What changes on a small screen is how the layout gives way, what the device does with the page, two notices that only appear on a phone or a tablet, and what the browser keeps so that the page can open at all when the server cannot be reached.

How the layout gives way

The page asks to be as wide as the device it opened on, and its layout is fluid: nothing about it becomes a second design at a phone's width. Each row of the display is a line with its readings pushed out to its two ends, so a narrow screen narrows the space between those ends rather than moving readings onto new lines, and a reading too long for its cell is cut by the cell rather than wrapped. The nine control buttons stay three rows of three at every width. They only get narrower, and their labels are what give way, so a two-word label takes two lines.

The two panels are where the layout genuinely changes shape, because their contents are laid out by count and that count depends on the width available.

Putting it on a home screen

The application declares itself as something a device can install. Its manifest gives it the name Rdio Scanner, declares it a standalone application — the device is asked to open it as its own application rather than as one more page in a browser tab — asks to be kept upright, gives its background colour and its theme colour the same dark grey the page's own chrome is built with, and carries its icon at sizes from 72 to 512 pixels so that the device has one to draw whatever shape it needs. The page also carries the icon and the status-bar setting an Apple device uses when a web page is added to a home screen.

How the adding happens is the browser's business rather than the application's: the wording, the moment, and whether it is offered at all belong to the browser, and the listening screen carries no install control of its own. What does belong to the application is worth knowing before you do it.

A shortcut opens the plain address

The manifest tells the device to open the application at its own plain address. If you gave a page a profile of its own with ?id=, its shortcut does not carry that profile: the installed icon opens the default profile, which is the one the browser remembers for the plain address. The stored access code behaves the same way and worse, because it belongs to the browser rather than to any profile, as the chapter on access codes explains.

The notice about the native applications

Ten seconds after the page opens, on a phone or a tablet, the application shows a notice of its own about the native applications. It appears when what the browser reports about the device says Android, iPad, or iPhone — which is why it never appears in a browser on a desktop computer, and why nothing you do on the page can make it appear there either.

The sentence it shows is built with the platform's own word in the middle: Do you know that Rdio Scanner works natively on Android devices?, or the same sentence with Apple in that place. Beside it sits a badge that points at the store page for that platform's native application — Google Play on Android, the App Store on the Apple one — and a countdown that runs down from ten, and the end of that countdown is what closes the notice. The notice records nothing about having been shown, offers no way to decline it for good, and comes back ten seconds after every time the page is opened.

That is as far as this guide goes with the native applications. They exist, for Android and for Apple devices; their screens are not documented here, and this guide links to neither store. What the notice does is point at the store's own page for the application belonging to that platform, which is where the comparison between the webapp and the native application has to be made.

What that notice is like with a screen reader

It arrives by itself, ten seconds in, while you are doing something else, in a region the page reports as a status. The countdown is written into the same block of text as the sentence, so a reading of the notice gives a bare digit run onto the end of the sentence rather than a sentence and then a number, and that digit rewrites itself once a second. There is no dismiss control anywhere in the notice: the countdown is what closes it, and the only thing in it the keyboard can act on is the badge — which is a picture, and the link it sits inside is given no name for a screen reader to read. On the Apple form the picture does have a name, but it is the name of the file the badge was drawn from rather than something a person would say. The chapter on keyboard and screen readers counts this among the application's accessibility findings, beside the ones it earns credit for.

When a new version is waiting

The page keeps its own files in the browser, and looks for a newer version of them every five minutes. When a newer one has been published the browser takes it in the background and holds it waiting: nothing on the screen changes while you are listening, because a replacement does not take over a page that is in use. Telling the open pages is something the browser does only when it is replacing a version that is in charge, which is why opening a server for the first time never brings this prompt.

When a newer version is waiting, the page opens a dialog titled New version available, reading A new version of the scanner webapp is available. Would you like to reload the page?, with No and Yes beside it. It appears once each time the page is opened.

Yes puts the new version in charge and reloads the page, which ends whatever the old one was doing. No leaves the version you are on running; the newer one goes on waiting, and takes over when the page is opened again with no copy of the running version still open. The consequence is worth holding on to: the version you are listening on is the version your browser has, which is not necessarily the version the server is running, and that dialog is how you are told the two have diverged.

With the server gone

Because the page keeps its own files, it opens even when the server cannot be reached. That is worth knowing in both directions: the page coming up is not evidence that the server is working, and the parts of the screen that fill themselves in are not evidence that there is anything to see.

9 · Help and support

A listener cannot configure anything about support. The name on the screen, the address the screen will offer, and the version the screen reports are all decided by the deployment and by the build the page came from. What a listener can do is read what the screen already says, know when the screen has nothing to offer, and say the right things when asking somebody for help.

Which deployment this is

The line across the top of the screen is the name the operator gave the deployment. It is not a name the listener chooses, changes, or can add, and a deployment whose operator gave no name shows nothing there at all: an empty space with the light beside it. That is not the server failing, it is the deployment not having a name to show. Beside the name sits the light that says whether the page is joined to the server, and that light carries its state in its name as well as in its colour — a reader who cannot see the colour is told Server status and the state, and not only the state's colour.

When a deployment runs more than one server, or when the same person listens to deployments run by different people, the name on the screen is the only thing that tells the two apart. It is the first thing worth quoting when something is wrong, and if the line is empty, quote the address you were given instead.

The help control, and when the screen has none

A deployment whose operator configured a contact address gets one more control in the corner of the screen, named Help. Press it and a notice appears along the bottom of the screen: the sentence For further assistance please contact, then the deployment's own address, written so that you can act on it — pressing it starts a mail message addressed to that address, in a new window rather than by taking you away from the screen you were watching. The notice counts down from ten, one a second, and the end of the countdown closes it: the address is on the screen for about ten seconds, and if you needed longer than that to copy it down, the notice has gone and the control has to be pressed again.

A deployment whose operator did not configure an address gets no Help control at all. Nothing on the screen says so, and nothing on the screen offers a way to find out who runs it: the control that would have shown the notice is the thing that is missing. The application does have a sentence ready for that case — it would point at the project's own discussions, named Rdio Scanner discussions — but the control that shows the notice is hidden in exactly the situation where that sentence is the one the application would need to say, so there is no press that reaches it. If your screen has no help control, the operator who gave you the address is the way to get help, and the discussions are where the application itself would have sent you.

The version line is the page's version

The large row of the display carries the display's own name until a call arrives on it, and that name is Rdio Scanner v followed by a version. That version was stamped into the page when the page was built, and it belongs to the page and to your browser. A release build stamps the release and a mark naming the commit the build was made from, so a released line reads something like 8.0.0 with a commit mark after it. A build made without the release tool stamps the version the repository declares, or dev — and a version line that says dev is a page somebody built from a source tree, which no release ever had.

The server does report which server it is, and the page uses that report for two things only: the name across the top of the screen and the contact address. The version the server reports is not shown anywhere on the listening screen. So when you and the operator are quoting versions at each other, quote both and say which is which — the version line on your screen is the build your browser has, and the version the operator quotes from the server is a different thing, as the chapter on phones and tablets explains for the case where your browser has an older build than the deployment.

10 · Keyboard and screen readers

The listening screen grew out of a version that carried none of this, and what it now carries for a keyboard and a screen reader is worth knowing in both directions. There is more on the page than what you can see. And some of what you can see is the only place a piece of information lives. This chapter gives both, and the colour figures behind them: the guide measured the listening screens against WCAG 2.2 at its AA conformance level, and the figures here come from the colours the shipped page declares rather than from judging a picture of it.

Getting onto the page, and what is on it

The first thing the page offers is a link named Skip to contents, ahead of everything else, and it moves the keyboard to the part of the page that holds the screen. That part carries the name Rdio Scanner, given to it by a heading the page keeps hidden from the eye and hands to a screen reader — which is what lets a reader move by landmark and land on the listening screen by name instead of by counting positions. Each panel is a landmark of its own, and the landmark carries no name of its own: what says which panel a panel is, is a heading the page keeps hidden from the eye — Search and Select — and the close control, Close search panel and Close select panel, which names the panel by saying what pressing it does. A reader who moves from heading to heading finds both panels by name; a reader who moves from landmark to landmark is handed two regions with nothing on them saying which is which.

Where the keyboard goes when a panel opens, and where it comes back

A drawer that slides out under a press and leaves the keyboard sitting on the control that opened it puts the panel behind the reader in the tab order, so the page marks a landing place inside each panel. Opening one moves the keyboard to that marked control. A panel that marks nothing takes the first control its own content offers, and a panel with nothing to work in takes the control that closes it. Closing a panel puts the keyboard back on the control that opened it, because focus left standing on a control that has just left the page lands the reader at the top of the document.

The marked landing place matters because a panel rebuilds its own content while the page answers. The search panel repaints every row of its table on each answer, so a keyboard standing on a row is standing on a control the answer takes away, and the browser puts the reader at the top of the page. The control a panel marks as its landing place is one its own answers leave standing.

Every control the keyboard can reach draws a ring around itself when the keyboard arrives, including the panel close controls, the help control, the full-screen control, and the small controls in the search panel. The ring is drawn outside the control rather than over it, which is what keeps a control's own face readable.

What the page says out loud

The page carries one live region, and it is kept empty until something happens: a region that holds permanent text tells a screen reader the page is answering every time it paints. A sentence is written into it only when the thing that sentence names changes, and the first painting of a page is silent. It holds one sentence at a time, and a new writing replaces it, so two things changing in the same beat are given as the later one. These are the sentences, in the application's own words:

  • Queue: and a number, when the queue of calls waiting to be heard changes.
  • Playing or Paused and the talkgroup's label, when the audio in front of you starts or stops for a reason you caused.
  • Stopped and the name the display was showing, when a call ends.
  • Searching calls, when a search begins, and then the number of calls the answer found.
  • Page and a number, when the results are turned to another page.

The notices the page shows along its bottom — the two described in the chapter on phones and tablets and the chapter on help and support — are a separate announcement surface, and neither carries a dismiss control. Each one's countdown is a bare number written into the same block of text as its sentence and rewritten once a second, which is why the countdown reads as part of the sentence rather than as a number after it.

The recoloured reading

Some readers set their device to draw everything in the system's own colours instead of the page's. Under that reading the page wears the system's canvas and ink, and the parts of it that carry information keep a boundary drawn in that ink: the display area, every control, the server light as a filled square whose name still says which state it is in, and the keyboard's ring in the system's own highlight colour. What does not survive is the small state lights in the corners of the controls, which are taken by the mode outright. That is the clearest case in this chapter of the difference the table above names: where the state was also given in words it survives, and where it was only ever a colour it does not.

Motion, and who can turn it off

The page has motion. The server light blinks while audio is held. A talkgroup's button in the select panel blinks in its corner while that talkgroup is held off for a set window of minutes. The search panel shows a progress bar moving while an answer is on its way, and a spinning reload icon in a row while that row's recording is being fetched. The drawers slide open and shut. There is no control anywhere on the screen that turns any of it off. What answers is the reader's own device: the page carries a rule for the operating system's setting for reduced motion, and under it every one of those animations stops, the drawers and the sliding bars arrive already at the state they were heading to, and the paused light gains the standing mark described above instead of a blink. A reader who needs that has to ask the device for it, and cannot ask the page.

Sizes, narrow widths, and zoom

The smallest pressable things on the screen are the round controls in the search panel — the play, stop, and download controls in its rows, and the four controls that turn its results to another page — and they are 48 CSS pixels across. The nine controls under the display are 40 pixels tall. The standard's floor for a target is 24 by 24, and nothing a listener presses is under it. A control the page has itself switched off is a different matter: when there is no further page to turn to, the paginator's four controls are dimmed to 38 percent of the colour its own words are painted in, and the standard asks nothing of a control that is inactive.

The page sets the width it uses to the device it opened on, and sets no ceiling on the device's own zoom, which is what a reader who needs larger text depends on. At 320 CSS pixels — the narrowest width the application claims, and the one it refuses to go below — everything a listener needs is still on the page, the controls are still three rows of three, and nothing runs off the side. Two readings are trimmed by the boxes they sit in. The display's rows are cut rather than wrapped, and here that is what costs: the deployment's name ends in an ellipsis, which at least says that something was cut, and the version line in the large row is cut off partway with no mark at all. Both readings matter when a listener reports a problem, and the chapter on help and support says what to say when they will not fit.

Motion is answered only by the reader's device. The page offers no control for it. The page does answer that setting, and answers it by replacing motion with a standing mark rather than by switching the marked state off.
At 320 CSS pixels the deployment's name is cut with an ellipsis and the version line is cut with no mark to say it happened. The narrow width costs nothing else, and the help chapter says what to report when those two readings will not fit.

What the application does well is worth saying once plainly, because most of it was a deliberate departure from the version the screen grew out of: the skip link and the named landmark, the landing place inside a panel and the return to the control that opened it, the focus ring on every control the keyboard reaches, the announcements that speak only when something named has changed, the state carried in words where a screen reader needs it, the marked controls and marked results instead of removed ones, the quieted motion that keeps the state motion was carrying, and the one-character font rule that keeps the hourglass a mark. None of that is a courtesy: it is the part of the screen that works when a pointer is not being used, and it is why the faults above can be named against an answer instead of against nothing.

This guide, and the same rules

The guide carries the two device rules in its own stylesheet: under a request for reduced motion its motion stops, and under a forced colours reading it draws itself in the system's colours rather than its own. Its colours are measured against the same floors as the page's, its figures are given a name and a sentence, no meaning in it is carried by colour alone, and it links to nothing outside itself. Where it describes a fault above it does not soften it, and where it names a figure it names the figure that was measured.

11 · When something does not work

This chapter is arranged the way a listener arrives at it: with the symptom, not with the part of the application that causes it. Each entry says what the screen is doing, what you can do about it with the controls the screen already gives you, and where the answer is not yours to reach. Nothing in this chapter is a procedure for the machine behind the deployment. Those procedures are written in the operator's guide, and the line between the two documents is the line this guide has been drawing since its first chapter.

Two readings do most of the work here, and both are already on the screen. One is the server light, which carries its state in its own name: a screen reader is told Server status: followed by the state it is in. The other is the link reading, the part of the display that says whether the page is joined to the server, and it reads NO LINK while it is not. A good deal of what looks like silence is that one reading, and it is worth learning what it does and does not mean.

A talkgroup is silent while the others play

Three things make one talkgroup quiet while the rest carry on, and all three are states the listener set at some point. The talkgroup is not chosen, which SELECT TG shows you directly — the chapter on choosing what to hear gives what a button reads when it is chosen, when it is held off, and when it is neither. The talkgroup is being held off, which is what AVOID arms: while a window of minutes is standing, the flag row carries the hourglass with the minutes that window is held for, and that talkgroup's own button in the select panel carries the timed mark. The whole system, or the talkgroup alone, is being held, which is HOLD SYS or HOLD TG switched on.

Start in the select panel, because it is the one place these states are written in words as well as in colour. The four controls with a state carry their state in colour alone, with no words on the control and no sentence written out loud for it; the chapter on keyboards and screen readers sets out what each of them offers a reader who cannot rely on the colour. Pressing AVOID again steps the hold forward, and the cycle the chapter on live calls gives comes back around to no hold at all, which is how an armed window is let go.

When a talkgroup is chosen, not held, and not held off, and it is still silent, the deployment is not hearing it. Ask the operator, who is the one who sets which talkgroups a site listens to.

Nothing is arriving at all

Work outward from the screen's own readings: when nothing is arriving, they say whether the page is asking for nothing or receiving nothing. If the link reading says NO LINK, the page has no connection to the server, and no call can reach it while that is true; the chapter on access codes gives what the page does with the code it stored in the browser between visits, and the field under the display takes another attempt at it. If that reading says something else, the four controls with a state are the next thing to read: LIVE FEED switched off asks the application for nothing at all, HOLD SYS and HOLD TG switched on hold what would otherwise reach you, and PAUSE holds the audio while the page goes on showing what it last showed.

The server light tells you which of these the page thinks it is in, and it carries that in its own name as well as in its colour: a screen reader is told Server status: followed by which state it is in. A dark light and that name reading off together mean the page is holding nothing in front of you, which is not the same as the deployment having nothing to send. Ask the operator whether the server is receiving calls; that is a fact about the sites, and it is not something a listener's screen can show.

A search answers nothing

The search panel reads the calls the server has stored, and not the calls it is hearing right now. So a search that answers nothing has two possible causes, and the panel tells you which of them it is by the reading it gives: an empty answer reads 0 of 0 in its range, and that reading is about the filters you have asked with, not about the server being out of calls. The control named Reset clears the filters — while an answer is on its way the panel marks that control as doing nothing, because there is no answer to change yet — and the chapter on searching gives each filter, what it holds, and how the dates bound the answer. The control SEARCH CALL opens the panel and runs the search with the filters it is already holding, which is the quickest way to tell an empty filter apart from a deployment that has nothing to give.

Audio will not pause, or will not start

PAUSE holds the recording that is in front of you where it is: it suspends the audio in the middle of the call and freezes the clock that has been running on it, and letting go of the hold continues where it stopped, in the same call at the same place. What it does not do is start anything. While the hold is standing, the application begins no new audio at all — not a call that arrives, and not a call you asked to hear again from REPLAY LAST or from a search result. If a replay seems to do nothing, look at the paused state first. The control's own corner light carries it however audio was left, and the server light's name carries it when a recording was in flight when you pressed: the chapter on live calls gives which is which, and what pressing the control again puts back.

Audio will not stop

A hold is not a stop, and a listener who expects PAUSE to end the call is left with a screen that looks stuck while it is doing exactly what it was asked to do. To move on, press SKIP NEXT: it ends what is playing, and while a hold is standing that is all it does — the next thing the queue holds starts only when you let go of the hold. Letting go of a hold you have not skipped from continues the same recording where it stopped. To end the queue as well, press LIVE FEED while it is switched on — the queue is emptied, the audio stops, and the light on that control reads the colour of off. REPLAY LAST then plays the last call the display showed again, which is the way back to something you have already heard once.

The live feed does not come back from one press

After a playback you opened from the search panel, the page is working in its playback mode, and that is a third state the control has: its light reads the colour of a partial state rather than on or off. Pressing LIVE FEED once from there ends the replay and leaves the live feed switched off, which is not what a listener usually meant. It takes twice: the first press ends the replay, the second asks for the live feed again. The chapter on searching recorded calls explains how a playback leaves the page, and this is the one symptom whose remedy is simply a second press.

The timed avoid mark arrives as a box

The hourglass that stands beside the minutes of an avoid window is a character the page asks a font for, not a drawing, and on a machine with none of the fonts it asks for it can arrive as a box or as nothing at all. The reading it carries does not change: the minutes are still beside it, and the flag row still says the talkgroup is avoided. There is nothing for a listener to install or change. The chapter on what the display says names the mark and the fonts it asks for.

A call arrives flagged delayed

DELAYED in the flag row is not a fault and there is nothing for a listener to correct. It says the deployment holds this call's audio back before anyone is told about it, counted in whole minutes. A talkgroup with a delay of its own decides first, and the system it belongs to is consulted only when the talkgroup names nothing. The minutes are the operator's to set or remove; ask the operator, and name the talkgroup. The chapter on what the display says gives the flag row reading in full.

A call you heard is not in the results

Two shipped rules account for a call that was heard and cannot be found again, and neither is a fault. The first is retention: the server prunes the calls and log entries that have outlived the retention its configuration sets, on an hourly schedule, and it reads the retention in force at the moment it runs. The setting the operator changes is named Prune Days, and it is the operator's to set; a call older than that is not in the results and no listener action brings it back. The chapter on searching recorded calls gives what that pass removes from a search, and the reference chapter lists the setting beside the others the operator sets. The second is duplicate detection: a call heard on the same system, the same talkgroup, and the same site within 500 milliseconds on either side of another is stored once, which is why the second copy of a call that arrived twice is not in the results either. The detection window is a shipped constant, and switching it off is the operator's setting, not a choice on the listening screen.

The unlock code is refused

The field under the display is named Unlock code, and when the server answers your attempt by asking for the code again the field says Invalid unlock code. That one sentence covers three situations and the screen does not say which one it is: the code was typed wrong, the attempts counted against your session and your address have run out, or a code that was valid arrived on the last attempt the server allows and was locked for that. The chapter on access codes gives how many attempts the server counts, over what window, and why clearing the page does not clear those counters. The field takes another attempt, so try the code once more exactly as the operator gave it; if it is refused again, ask the operator, who holds the code and knows what it was set to.

The code has expired, or too many connections

Two answers do say what they are. The field reads This unlock code has expired when the code's own end date has passed, and there is nothing a listener can do about that: the length a code lives for is the operator's to set, and a new code is what resolves it. The field reads Too many connections when the limit that belongs to the code itself has been reached, which is a different limit from the number of listeners the server allows at all. That larger cap is named Max Clients in the operator's settings, and reaching it does not print anything on your screen — the page keeps NO LINK, which is why a full deployment and a lost connection look the same from the listener's side. The chapter on access codes works through both, including what happens to a code the moment a connection is lost.

A selection seems lost

What you have chosen lives in the browser, not on the server. It is stored per profile, and a profile is decided by the ?id= marker on the address you opened, so a page opened without the marker, or with a different one, reads a different selection and looks as though yours was lost. It also survives an update until that profile changes something, after which the older shape of it is replaced. The chapter on choosing what to hear gives what is stored and where. SELECT TG shows what the page is holding now, and choosing again writes it. The reset control /reset clears everything the page stored in the browser — your code as well as your selection — so use it when you want a clean start and not when you are trying to find your choices.

An old version is still on the screen

The page keeps its own files in the browser and looks for a newer set of them every five minutes. When a newer set is waiting, the page opens a dialog titled New version available and asks whether it may reload the page; the answer that says yes takes the new files and reopens the page, and the other answer leaves what you have running in place. The dialog appears only when an installation is waiting to replace the one in control, which is why a first visit to a page never gets it. What the page keeps in your browser, and what a newer set of files does with what an older application left there, is set out in the chapter on phones and tablets, and what the version update itself costs a listener is in the opening chapter.

What to bring when the answer is not yours

Several of the entries above end the same way, and that is not the guide running out of things to say: the deployment's configuration, the sites it listens to, the retention it keeps calls for, the limits on a code, and the delay on a talkgroup are all set by the operator. What you can bring is concrete — the version the page prints in its own line at the bottom of the display, the talkgroup and system, the time on the call you were looking at, what the controls' own states were, and what the link reading was saying. The chapter on help and support says where to take it and what the application can tell you about itself.
